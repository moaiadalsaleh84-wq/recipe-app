# How to Design an App So Addictive That People Can't Stop Using It

*The owner's note: a video transcript about the habit framework behind Duolingo, TikTok and Tinder. The words are as given.*

## Short summary (for Claude)

A six-step framework for getting people to come back:

1. **The itch (trigger):** apps get opened because of a feeling (bored, curious, hungry), not just notifications. Tie the app to that feeling, and write notifications that speak to it ("someone liked you"), never generic ("we miss you"). Remind people at the moment they feel it.
2. **The lazy thumb:** make the core action one step. Make it the biggest thing on screen, at the bottom where the thumb rests. Lower the bar for success (Duolingo: one lesson keeps the streak, which raised retention 3.3%).
3. **The paved path:** people take the default. Open on the one action that matters, not a menu; give every choice a good default.
4. **The slot machine:** unpredictable rewards keep people engaged longer than predictable ones (Skinner's pigeons, Tinder's swipe).
4.5. **The bottomless pit:** remove dead ends. Empty searches, list ends and error pages should always lead somewhere next.
   *(The video goes from step 4.5 straight to step 6.)*
6. **The piggy bank:** every use leaves something behind (taste, history, progress), so the app gets more personal and leaving costs something.

**How it fits Pantry Pop**, within `DESIGN.md` and `CLAUDE.md`:

- **Fits well, much of it already in the app:**
  - the dinner reminder tied to the "what's for dinner?" moment;
  - Tonight's pick as the one default action;
  - "Cook it!" as the big bottom button;
  - streaks that count any cooked meal;
  - no dead ends (empty search, list and history all offer a next step);
  - small surprises after cooking (the lucky pan bonus, stickers, chef tips);
  - personal history, ratings, cookbooks and pantry.
- **Doesn't fit, so don't build it:**
  - guilt or loss pressure ("you'll lose your streak", "we miss you");
  - fake urgency;
  - endless feeds built to stop people leaving;
  - reward systems built to feel like gambling.

  `DESIGN.md` says: *encouraging, never guilt or loss warnings*, and *honest: no fake timers, invented reviews, pretend data*. The goal is that people come back because the app helps them cook, not because it's hard to stop.

## The note

Duolingo, TikTok, and Tinder all have one thing in common, and it's the reason people keep coming back to them every single day without even thinking about it.

Because it doesn't really matter how beautiful your app looks. If people stop using it on day one, all your hard work goes to waste.

I've designed some of the most popular apps on the App Store, and I've seen firsthand the psychological tricks you need to use to make people come back every day.

So in this video, I'm going to explain the science behind why these apps are so addictive that people can't stop using them.

All of these apps use the same six-step framework. It starts before the user even opens the app and continues until they come back every single day.

Let's go through these steps together, and I'll explain how they work and how you can apply them to your own app.

Every app struggles with getting users to open it. The average person has 93 apps on their phone, but only opens about half of them in any given month. The rest just sit there unused.

But think about the last time you opened TikTok. Chances are, it wasn't because you received a notification. You were simply bored, felt an urge, and before you even thought about it, your thumb was already hovering over the app icon.

That urge is what I call the itch.

So how does an app become an itch?

There's a great book called Hooked that categorizes the reasons people open apps into two categories.

First, there are external triggers. These are things like notifications or the red badges on your app icon.

Second, there are internal triggers, which are emotions.

For example, you might feel bored, curious, or feel like you're falling behind in learning Spanish.

And most of the time, internal triggers are what make you open an app.

So when you're bored, you open TikTok.

But let's look at the opposite example.

Let's say you downloaded a meditation app in January. On day one, you actually meditated for a few minutes, felt great, and everything was going well.

But by day three, you had already forgotten the app even existed.

Forgetting about the app doesn't mean you stopped feeling stressed or experiencing whatever internal trigger made you download the meditation app in the first place.

It simply means the app failed to connect itself to that feeling.

And I bet your own app is making the same mistake.

Take a look at the copy in your app and the notifications you send to people.

Are they generic messages like "We miss you", or do they actually speak to the user's emotions?

The best apps start with external triggers, and over time, they become associated with a specific feeling in the user's mind.

That's why you don't need a notification to open TikTok. Boredom itself becomes the notification.

Here's how to apply this trigger to your own app.

First, write down the feeling your app satisfies. It could be boredom, curiosity, or excitement.

Next, write down the notifications you could send people that speak directly to that feeling.

For example, dating apps tell you that someone liked you. They don't say, "Keep using our app." That's just boring corporate language. Nobody cares about that.

And finally, make sure you understand when your users experience that feeling, and remind them that your app exists when they experience it.

For example, if someone gets bored during their commute and your app solves boredom, make sure you remind them about your app while they're commuting.

But this trigger only gets people to open your app. What happens next determines whether they actually start using it or not.

And that's what I call the lazy thumb.

B.J. Fogg runs the Behavior Design Lab at Stanford University, and he has a very simple way of explaining this.

Your motivation to do something rises and falls throughout the day, and you can't really control it.

But what you can control is how easy it is to take action.

So instead of waiting for your users to have a huge amount of motivation to do something, give them something easier to do, so they can complete it even when their motivation is low.

Let's say you're trying to track what you eat. You've just had lunch and want to log it.

When I analyzed the calorie-tracking app Yazio in episode 60 of App Teardown, I couldn't even log my food from the home screen with one or two taps.

I had to scroll, enter a menu, and then type in the information.

Now compare that to their competitor, Cal AI, where you simply take a picture of your plate, and you're done.

That's the entire product experience.

Both apps do exactly the same thing. They accomplish the same task, but one requires multiple steps while the other only requires a single picture.

As a result, the easier app received more than 15 million downloads in two years.

And notice how all the most addictive apps make their core action incredibly simple.

TikTok: one swipe.

Tinder: one swipe.

Duolingo: one tap on a huge button at the bottom of the screen to start a new lesson.

To apply this to your own app, first identify its core action.

Then count how many steps it takes to complete that action and reduce them to just one step, with absolutely no friction.

Once you've identified this action, make the button that performs it the biggest element on the screen and place it at the bottom, where the thumb naturally rests.

Because half of your app's users will be using their phones with one hand.

Make it easy to reach.

Third, lower the bar for what counts as an accomplishment.

For example, Duolingo used to count your streak only if you completed your entire daily goal.

Then they changed it so your streak would still count even if you completed just one lesson.

As a result, user retention increased by 3.3%.

But sometimes even one tap is too much if it's difficult to figure out what you're supposed to tap.

You might think people prefer making their own choices, right?

But most of the time, users simply go with whatever has already been selected for them.

The most famous example of this is a 2003 study about organ donation in Europe.

In some countries, you have to actively choose to donate your organs.

As a result, only around 23% of people donate their organs.

But in other countries, you have to actively choose NOT to donate your organs.

As a result, around 86% of the population donates their organs.

So even though it's the exact same decision, there's a massive difference in what people choose, simply because some countries make one option the default, while other countries make the opposite option the default.

Going against the default requires effort, so people simply don't do it.

If people follow the default option for something as important as organ donation, imagine how little effort they'll put into going against the default when it comes to something as simple as choosing a button in an app.

That's why TikTok never asks you what you want to watch. It just starts automatically.

Duolingo doesn't give you endless choices. It shows you the next lesson and highlights it.

Tinder doesn't ask you who you want to see next. It simply shows you someone.

This is what I call the paved path: choosing the next option for the user without overwhelming them with decisions.

So how can you create a paved path in your own app?

First, take the action you identified under the lazy thumb and make it the first thing users see when they open your app.

When a user opens your app, they shouldn't see a menu or a bunch of different options. They should see the one option that matters most.

Next, go through your app and identify every place where you're making users make a decision.

For every decision you ask them to make, make sure there's a default option.

That default option should be the best choice for both you and your user.

And you'll notice that most of the time, people tend to choose that default option.

So those are the first three tricks that addictive apps use to pull you in:

A trigger that makes you open the app, an extremely easy first action, and a predefined path to follow.

But getting people into your app is only half the battle. Now you need to make them addicted.

And that's where the next three steps come in, backed by TikTok's own internal documents.

In 2024, a lawsuit in Kentucky accidentally revealed some of TikTok's internal documents.

In those documents, TikTok gave a name to the moment when someone becomes hooked on the app.

They call it the "Habit Moment", and it happens exactly after you've watched 260 videos.

Now, how do they get you to watch that many videos so quickly?

The reason this works goes back to a scientist named B.F. Skinner in the 1950s and his experiments with pigeons.

What he did was separate two groups of pigeons and give each group a button.

Whenever they pecked the button, they received food.

The difference was that the first group received food every single time they pressed the button.

But the second group didn't know whether they would receive food when they pressed the button or not.

It was completely random.

So he ran this experiment for a while, and after some time, he simply stopped giving the pigeons food.

The first group, which had always received food, stopped pressing the button very quickly because they were no longer getting anything.

But the second group continued pressing the button for a much longer time because they never knew when they would receive food.

This variable reward was what kept them going, just like a slot machine.

And here's the shocking part.

One of Tinder's co-founders stated that this study was what inspired the swipe feature in Tinder.

Every swipe on TikTok is like pulling the lever on a slot machine.

And that's why I call step four the slot machine.

Now think about how regular apps give out rewards.

Let's say it's a fitness app.

You finish a workout, and you receive a badge.

You think it's amazing. It's your first badge.

Then you complete another workout and receive a second badge.

You don't really care as much this time.

By the time you reach your tenth badge, you know exactly what's going to happen, so your brain stops paying attention.

That's the slot machine effect.

Make rewards unpredictable, and vary both when they appear and how big they are.

To implement this, simply think about what the reward represents in your app and make it unpredictable.

Make users unsure of what to expect, but give them rewards frequently.

But unpredictable rewards only work if there's always another reward coming.

And who knows more about keeping people engaged than Aza Raskin, the designer who invented infinite scroll, and who estimates that 100,000 human lives are wasted scrolling every single day?

That's the power of removing the end of a page.

And that's why I call step 4.5 of the addiction framework the bottomless pit.

Most things in life come with stopping cues.

Like the end of the day, the end of a YouTube video, or the bottom of a page.

These are the moments when your brain asks: Should I keep going, or is it time to stop?

If you remove all of these stopping cues, the user has to make the decision to leave on their own.

And as we saw with the paved path, people usually don't make decisions for themselves.

So they simply keep going when you remove those stopping cues.

And most apps, without even realizing it, are designed to make users stop using them.

Every empty search screen, every dead end, and every place where you don't direct the user somewhere else represents an opportunity for their brain to ask: Should I stop now?

In contrast, addictive apps never show you an exit.

In Duolingo, when you finish a lesson, the biggest button is the one that starts another lesson.

On TikTok, there's no end to the feed.

On Tinder, there's no end to the stack of cards.

On Netflix, the next episode starts before you've even decided whether you want to watch it.

That's the bottomless pit.

Always give the user the next step, the next action, or the next episode, and never show them an exit.

So if you want to apply this to your own app, start by listing every dead end, the end of every list, empty search results, and error pages.

List everything that doesn't take the user somewhere else.

Then simply take that list and make those situations guide users somewhere.

If a search result is empty, show them something they might be looking for.

If they've reached the end of a list, show them something else they might be interested in.

If they've reached the end of an episode, show them what comes next.

But never tell them that it's over.

Now that your users are engaged and taking multiple actions, you just need to make sure they come back.

And that's where step six of the addiction framework comes in.

Netflix estimates that its recommendation algorithm saves the company $1 billion every year.

Why?

Because every show you watch on Netflix teaches the platform more about what you like to watch.

And by the time you've watched enough content, cancelling Netflix feels like starting from scratch.

You don't want to explain all over again what you like watching.

And most addictive apps work in exactly the same way.

Every time you use them, you leave a little piece of yourself behind.

You leave your taste, your playlists, your connections, and your friends.

So it feels like a piggy bank.

Every time you use the app, you put another little coin inside.

And the more you use it, the harder it becomes to simply walk away.

That's why the sixth and final step of the framework is called the piggy bank.

It's when an app asks you to invest a little bit of effort, time, preferences, and data.

As a result, the app becomes more personalized, and you become more connected to it.

Imagine starting TikTok again with a brand-new account.

Your current feed has learned from every single video you've watched.

So starting over feels like leaving a piece of yourself behind.

And every time someone uses an addictive app, the app becomes more personalized to them.

So take a look at your own app.

If someone uninstalled it today, would they actually lose anything?

And if the answer is no, users won't come back.

How do you fix this?

First, start tracking what your users do inside your app.

Every search they make, every button they press, and every piece of information they give you should be treated as extremely valuable because it teaches you what they're actually coming to your app for.

And that's what you should give them more of.

But first, you need to track it.

Second, once you understand what they're looking for, give them a way to build something around it.

Give them progress.

Give them levels.

Give them an algorithm that continuously gets better.

The possibilities are practically endless.

But give them something they can build on every single day, and make sure they're aware that they could lose it if they don't come back.
